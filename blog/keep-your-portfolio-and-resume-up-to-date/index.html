Your work deserves to be seen: keep your portfolio and résumé ready

Your next opportunity should not have to wait for you to find the evidence of what you can do.

By Seyi Abolarin · 19 September 2026 · 7 min read

Imagine getting this message tomorrow morning: “We have an opportunity that sounds like you. Can you send your résumé and a few examples of your work today?”

You know you can do the work. But your résumé ends two roles ago. Your strongest presentation is buried in a WhatsApp conversation. The project you are most proud of has no write-up, and the link you once shared no longer opens.

That gap between what you can do and what someone else can see is the reason I believe an up-to-date portfolio matters. Your professional record should be ready before an opportunity arrives.

Your résumé opens the conversation. Your portfolio gives it substance.

A résumé helps someone understand your experience quickly: your roles, responsibilities, qualifications and relevant achievements. A portfolio gives selected parts of that experience room to breathe. It explains a problem, the choices you made, your contribution and what happened next.

“Developed dashboards” is a résumé statement. A portfolio entry can explain who needed the dashboard, what decision it supported, how you checked the data and what you learned from the people using it. A screenshot helps, but the reasoning behind it is what makes the work meaningful.

Both deserve attention. A beautiful portfolio with an outdated résumé creates confusion; a strong résumé with no accessible examples leaves useful evidence unexplored. Keep dates, role titles and contact details consistent across both.

“Think process, not product.”

Austin Kleon, Show Your Work!

That short piece of advice is especially useful when your work cannot be reduced to a polished final image. Explaining how you approached a difficult problem can show judgement, collaboration and care that a finished output alone cannot communicate.

Your professional story needs to keep moving.

Today, an introduction may begin with a shared link: a colleague forwards your profile, a prospective client reads your article, or a recruiter visits your website before arranging a conversation. An old page can describe a version of you that you have already outgrown.

The World Economic Forum’s Future of Jobs Report 2025 reports that employers expect 39% of workers’ existing skill sets to change or become outdated between 2025 and 2030. This is a survey-based expectation, not a prediction about every individual. It does, however, give useful context for a practical question: does your professional record reflect what you can do now?

You might have moved from preparing reports to designing information systems, from supporting a class to developing a teaching programme, or from delivering tasks to supervising a team. If your profile still describes only the earlier work, readers have no reason to know how far you have grown.

A portfolio belongs in more professions than we think.

Portfolios are often associated with designers, photographers and developers. I see them more broadly: a curated record of practice. The format can be a website, a short PDF or a private collection shared when appropriate. You do not need to make every aspect of your work public to benefit from keeping one.

These are illustrative examples of what that evidence could look like:

A teacher or trainer

A lesson plan, an accessible learning resource and a reflection on how feedback improved the next session. Use permission-cleared or anonymised examples of learner work.

A health professional

A patient-education resource, a service-improvement poster or a public presentation. Describe your contribution without sharing identifiable patient information.

An accountant or operations professional

A reconciliation template using fictional data, a process map or a case study explaining how a recurring reporting problem was addressed.

A skilled tradesperson or entrepreneur

Permission-cleared project photographs, a clear account of the brief and the work delivered, and customer feedback you have permission to publish.

A researcher or MEAL professional

A published report, an assessment approach, a monitoring framework or a reflection on how evidence informed programme decisions.

A student or career changer

A coursework project, volunteer contribution or self-directed exercise. Label it honestly and explain what it demonstrates and what you would improve.

The point is to make relevant capabilities understandable. A portfolio does not replace professional registration, qualifications, references or formal recruitment requirements. Nor does everybody need an elaborate personal website. Start with a format you can maintain.

Show the thinking behind the work.

In my own work across Information Management, MEAL, Data Analytics and GIS, a software list tells only a small part of the story. Describing an automated beneficiary distribution workflow, a community-feedback system or geospatial support for profiling explains much more about the problems I help teams address.

For example, a case study about an automated distribution list can explain the programme need, the validation checks, the role of colleagues and how the output fits into delivery. It can do that without exposing beneficiary records or claiming a time saving that was never measured.

For each piece of work, answer five questions:

  1. What was the problem? Explain the setting and who needed help.
  2. What was your role? Separate your contribution from the team’s work and credit collaborators.
  3. What did you do, and why? Describe the method, constraints and important decisions in plain language.
  4. What evidence can you share? Use a permitted image, public report, sample or demonstration. Clearly label fictional data and reconstructions.
  5. What changed, and what did you learn? Use measured results where available. Otherwise describe the output and its intended use without inventing impact.

Three thoughtful examples can tell a stronger story than thirty unexplained attachments. Readers should quickly understand why you chose each piece and what it says about your current practice.

Share responsibly, especially when your work involves people.

Some of our most meaningful work is also the least suitable for public display. Permission, confidentiality and dignity still apply on a personal website. A dashboard containing names, a case record or a photograph from a sensitive setting is not automatically appropriate because you helped create it.

Use public materials, obtain permission where needed, and replace restricted information with clearly labelled demonstration data. Removing names alone may not make a record safe to publish. When an example cannot be shared, explain the approach at an appropriate level instead.

Make updating a habit you can actually keep.

“Share something small every day.”

Austin Kleon, Show Your Work!

I take this as encouragement to document work consistently, rather than a requirement to publish daily. A sustainable routine beats an ambitious website that stays untouched for years.

  • After a milestone: save a few notes about the problem, your contribution, feedback and any verified result. Record what you have permission to share.
  • Once a month: spend 30 minutes updating one useful item. Add a lesson, refresh a case study or remove a broken link.
  • Every quarter: review your résumé and portfolio together. Check dates, job titles, skills, downloads, mobile display and contact details.
  • Before an application or proposal: select the most relevant examples and tailor your résumé to the opportunity.

If you write regularly, give each article a clear title, a short preview, a date and a topic. Let the main blog page help readers choose; let the individual article page hold the detail. An organised archive makes a growing body of work easier to explore.

Start with one piece of work you can explain well.

You do not need a perfect career story to begin. Choose one project, lesson, service improvement or piece of research. Explain the problem and your contribution. Add evidence you are allowed to share. Then make sure your résumé reflects the same stage of your professional journey.

The goal is not to appear busy. It is to make your work understandable, your growth visible and the next conversation easier to begin.

Your next 30 minutes

Open your résumé. Correct one outdated detail. Choose one recent piece of work and write five sentences using the questions above. That is the beginning of a useful portfolio.
